## What is this course, and what will you get out of it?

***Tackling hard problems in physics***

Physics is centered around a few core principles which come up again and again in different contexts. This course aims to help you build up a toolbox of techniques for approaching hard problems. You'll develop general problem solving strategies, learn specific mathematical tools, and practice applying these skills to new situations. Most importantly, you'll gain exposure to a variety of problems (and how to solve them), which you will be able to draw upon as a reference when faced with new problems in your later classes.

We'll be focusing on learning physics by doing problems *purposefully*: building our way up to involved problems from core principles, and reflecting on what we can learn from each mistake, approach, and solution. The in-class worksheets will often introduce new ideas in this way, in order to guide you through the material and let you explore the underlying connections. By practicing physics with intent, you'll gain as much as possible from each problem.

***Learning how to study***

Another major goal of this course is to help you learn how to *study* for physics. College-level physics at Berkeley is hard, and likely very different from what you might have experienced in high school. We will talk about study strategies that have worked for us and our peers and provide a space for you to discover how you learn best. We are also big advocates for collaboration! It's tough (and often, impossible) to go through physics alone, and we'll encourage and facilitate community building within your cohort.

***Connecting the dots***

Finally, we want to help connect this class into the broader framework of physics. Sometimes, you may wonder how the different topics in mechanics fit together, or how they're relevant in future classes. To help with this, we will highlight the key topics in the class that will show up again and again in your physics career. We will also discuss the underlying structure to topics and alternate approaches to understanding them that are often dismissed from introductory physics classes.

***After this course, you should be able to***...

- Break down a complicated problem to understand what it is asking and come up with several potential processes to solve it
- Check the accuracy of your solutions to problems and understand what a solution should look like before solving for it
- Use your intuitive physical understanding in conjunction with your mathematical knowledge to interpret and solve physics problems
- Understand how the topics of Physics 5A connect to your future coursework and the underlying structure of physics
- Implement a set of study strategies for physics that work for you
- Utilize academic resources (such as the textbook, office hours, lecture, online references, peers, Mathematica/Python, etc.) to effectively learn new material

We hope that, by the end of this course, you'll feel more confident using a variety of techniques for approaching difficult problems, and that you'll have formed a community of peers within your cohort you feel comfortable collaborating and learning with!

## Topics covered

The course runs alongside Physics 5A/7A and covers roughly the following:

- Visualization & order of magnitude estimation
- Dimensional analysis & limiting cases
- Taylor series
- Coordinate systems, decomposing vectors & dot product
- Complex numbers & Euler's identity
- Reference frames & Newton's Laws
- Conservation laws & symmetry
- Fields, line integrals & work, conservative functions
- Data analysis & interpreting plots
- Gravitation and $$r^n$$ forces
- Vector algebra, revisited
- Building integrals
- Rotational vs. linear motion
- Center of mass & momentum flow
- Fluids & plasma
- Differential equations & phase space
- Minima of a potential & simple harmonic oscillators
- Complex exponential for differential equations
- Damped & driven oscillations, resonance
- Coupled oscillations & eigenvectors
- Waves & PDEs
- Noninertial reference frames
- Special relativity & special topic

## Philosophy

The course was designed for a few reasons:

**Intro mechanics courses are not thematically organized.** There are key themes students should take away about how physics works, but most people take away a vague sense that blocks can do weird things when connected with pulleys and inclined planes.

**Intro mechanics courses assume students know how physicists treat math.** Physicists treat math differently than mathematicians, and in ways that are very confusing to new students. Worse, intro mechanics classes often assume math background students don't have. Those gaps need to be addressed head on.

**Intro physics is hard, and students need more support.** Self-explanatory.

**Intro physics does not explain how physicists solve problems.** Students often are not told how physicists actually solve problems in intro classes. ('Draw a picture' is often relatively feeble advice. As a student once told me after drawing a square on a triangle to represent an inclined plane problem, 'Okay, now what?') They also often see professors 'guessing' an answer or jumping to a conclusion via 'symmetry' or similar, and feeling like this is dark magic because this is never explained. We try to make explicit these ideas: visualization, 'guessing' the answer, dimensional analysis, order of magnitude estimation, rough proportionalities, and, yes, drawing a picture (with the right information!).

**Intro physics isn't welcoming.** Students and professors alike often use words like 'trivial' or 'obvious' for things that aren't at all either. Physics culture can often praise the 'lone genius' when the reality is that physics is fundamentally collaborative. Worse, we know for a fact that students from underrepresented backgrounds are far more affected by this - see, for example, the work by [Professor Chandralekha Singh](https://www.physicsandastronomy.pitt.edu/people/chandralekha-singh) at University of Pittsburgh which notes that men who got C's in intro physics classes felt the same level of confidence in their ability to do physics as women who got A's. We believe that not only is physics collaborative, physics is stronger when it has a diversity of people with a diversity of ideas. The more we can do to provide people with a strong and supportive community in physics, the better the next generation of physicists will be, and the more exciting their ideas.

**Intro physics should be exciting!** Students are often somewhat bored of intro physics, and don't see how the topics are as 'cool' as modern physics (quantum! general relativity! particle physics!). However, intro mechanics covers foundational topics that can help explain the world students interact with every day. This is *exciting* and we want to transmit that excitement and sense of ability to ask a question about the world and answer it to our students.
